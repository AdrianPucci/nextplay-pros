1Motivation

Who has the problem. Two groups who should be meeting and aren't:

  • Athletes outside the spotlight. Retired pros, college stars, Olympians in smaller sports, and minor-leaguers. Very few college athletes ever reach a major pro league: the NCAA estimates about 1.5% of college football players and about 1% of men's basketball players are drafted by a major pro league [1]. Even those who make it have short careers. The players' union's often-quoted figure is about 3.3 years of experience, and the league's own estimate is about 6 years for players who make an opening-day roster [3]. That leaves a very large group of people with real stories and skills, and no agent working for them.
  • Bookers with small and mid-size budgets. Companies planning a sales kickoff, schools planning an assembly, nonprofits planning a gala, and local businesses planning an opening. They want a relatable athlete, nearby and within budget.

How they handle it today. Bookers search online, ask friends, or go through a speaker bureau. Bureaus focus on well-known names and typically take a 20–30% commission on the speaker's fee [2]. Athletes wait to be asked, rely on old coaches and social media messages, and are easy targets for "pay a fee first" scams.

The cost. Bookers spend hours searching and still end up with a generic speaker. Athletes lose income: on a $3,000 talk, a 20–30% commission is $600–$900 [2]. And the athletes most likely to be targeted by scams are the young and first-time ones who have the least support.

What Next Play does differently, and why it matters:

  1. It searches a real roster with a tool instead of answering from memory, so every suggestion can actually be booked. Python then removes any athlete the search didn't return, so the AI cannot invent a person.
  2. It ranks by story fit, not fame. A comeback story for a "bouncing back" keynote beats a bigger name with an unrelated story, and each match comes with a one-sentence reason.
  3. The budget is enforced in code, not in the prompt. No athlete above the booker's maximum fee can appear.
  4. The athlete controls access. Every request is screened for scams, and contact details sit in a vault that no AI agent can read. They unlock only for a booker whose request the athlete accepted.
  5. It writes the athlete's marketing for them. Story Builder turns rough notes into a profile using only facts the athlete gave and flags every claim to verify.

3The agents

Next Play has three AI agents on gpt-6-luna, plus plain-Python guards around them. The rule of the design: the AI understands, ranks, writes, and judges; code makes every decision that must never go wrong (who exists, the budget, who sees contact details).

How they're organized. The agents don't call each other; each one sits at a single point in the booking flow (find → request → profile), and Python passes data between them. This keeps every agent small and testable, and means a failure in one can't cascade.

Models used and why. All three agents use gpt-6-luna, the model allowed by the course. It handles structured output and tool calls well, it's fast (Gatekeeper answers in about 2 seconds), and it's cheap ($0.10 per million input tokens, $0.01 cached, $0.50 output), which matters for an app that screens every message. The app first ran through the course's Portkey key. After that key was turned off (Nov 22), the app was switched to my own OpenAI key running the same model; the code supports both (MODEL_PROVIDER).

One real run, step by step

The booker typed: “Keynote for 250 credit-union employees in Denver in January about bouncing back from setbacks. Budget $5,000.” (run d7b197badc in output/audit_trail.json).

  1. Python input guard. The text is checked for injection and contact-harvesting phrases (none found), and any emails or phone numbers are removed. It's then wrapped in <untrusted> markers so the model treats it as data.
  2. Scout reads the request and fills its brief: event keynote, city Denver, audience 250 credit-union employees, budget $5,000, themes bouncing back from setbacks, resilience.
  3. Scout calls its tool: search_athletes(categories=['speaking'], near_city=Denver, max_fee=5000, keywords=['resilience', 'setbacks', 'comeback', 'bouncing back'])
    Python applies the filters (budget is capped in code even if the model asked for more) and returns 12 athletes with public fields only. No contact details exist in what the model sees.
  4. Scout ranks and explains the best fits (below), and asks one follow-up question: “What year and specific January date are you considering? Matches shown have minimum fees within the $5,000 budget.”
  5. Python output check: every returned ID was in the search results and every minimum fee was under $5,000, so nothing was dropped.
  6. Glass box: the result shows 2 model calls, 5,098 input tokens (2,750 from cache), 541 output tokens (157 reasoning), $0.00059, 9.8 seconds.

Cost-cutting steps, with evidence

  1. Prompt caching (measured). At first nothing was cached: each agent's fixed instructions were under the provider's 1,024-token minimum, and Scout's later calls were unique because of random tool-call IDs. I moved all variable text after an explicit cache breakpoint and put useful content into the fixed part (ranking rules and a worked example for Scout, a scam playbook for Gatekeeper, a style guide for Story Builder). Scout's measured cost per search went from $0.00077 (22% cached) to $0.00039 (82% cached) on repeated test searches, a 50% cut. Gatekeeper and Story Builder went from 0% cached to about 85%.
  2. Block before the model. Blunt injection and contact-harvesting requests are stopped by Python rules and never reach the model: 0 tokens, $0 (see the Glass box in the Safety screenshot).
  3. Small, structured outputs. Every agent returns a fixed schema instead of free text, which keeps output (the most expensive token type) to a few hundred tokens.
  4. Limits. Each run is capped at 6 model calls and 4 tool calls, and the app stops all AI runs once a daily spend cap (default $2) is reached.

Trade-off, with evidence. Making the instructions long enough to cache made the first call of the day more expensive: writing to cache costs $0.125/M instead of $0.1/M, and Scout still writes about 2,017 tokens per run, because each run's tool results are new. That's why live Scout runs average $0.00059 ($0.00049–$0.00076) rather than the $0.00039 seen on repeated test searches. The longer instructions also made answers better: Scout now writes story-specific reasons and asks a useful follow-up question, and Gatekeeper stopped flagging normal low-budget school requests (test G4).

7Limits and next steps

What doesn't work yet

  • The roster is fictional. All 40 athletes and their contacts are made up for the demo. Recruiting real athletes is the first business step.
  • No real accounts. Demo sign-in is a picker; access rules are enforced on the server, but there are no passwords or verified identities.
  • No payments, contracts, or calendars. After "accept," the two sides arrange the details off the platform.
  • Scout's live cost varies ($0.00049–$0.00076) because each run writes its tool results to cache.
  • Impact numbers are estimates until real users have been measured.

What I'd build next

  1. Real-user pilot with athletes and bookers from my own network: time saved, bookings made, scam attempts caught.
  2. Verified athlete identities and athlete-set availability calendars.
  3. An Opportunity Matcher agent that alerts athletes to posted gigs, and an Outreach Drafter that helps bookers write strong requests.
  4. Payments with escrow, so neither side carries the risk of a no-show.
  5. A full Comedy & Entertainment section (hosts, emcees, roasts).
